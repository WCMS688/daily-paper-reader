# A Composable AI-Accelerated Iterative Solver for 3D-IC Thermal Modeling 

Yixing Li, Jiahang Zhou, Zhiyu Zeng, Xin Ai 

Abstract —Accurate thermal analysis of heterogeneous 2.5D/3D-IC packages is essential yet computationally prohibitive. A single full-package FEM simulation can take hours, while AI-based surrogates treat the entire stack as a monolithic prediction target and must be retrained whenever the die count or topology changes. To address this limitation, this work proposes Domain-Decomposed AI-Accelerated Iterative Solver for Thermal Analysis (DAIST), a composable thermal solver that decomposes the global package simulation into block-level subdomain problems, replaces subdomain solvers with neural operators, and couples them through iterative exchanges of interfacial temperature and heat flux. This local-to-global architecture eliminates the topology lock-in of monolithic models: block-level neural operators can be directly reused in unseen package assemblies without retraining. The iterative coupling strategy further provides a controllable accuracy–runtime tradeoff, where the iteration budget can be adjusted to trade accuracy for runtime. Evaluated on amulti-chiplet system and an advanced packaging system, DAIST achieves up to 178 × speedup over traditional FEM solvers with mean temperature errors of 0.068% and 0.323%, respectively, while demonstrating cross-topology reuse of block-level models across structurally distinct package assemblies. 

Index Terms —3D-IC, thermal simulation, domain decomposi-tion, operator learning 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Thermal analysis has become increasingly critical in modern heterogeneous 2.5D and 3D integrated packages. Unlike tradi-tional monolithic single-die designs, contemporary package ar-chitectures consist of multiple thermally coupled components such as logic dies, high-bandwidth memory (HBM) stacks, interposers, and interface materials [1], [2]. Heat generated in one component can propagate across neighboring blocks through physical contact regions, creating complex thermal interactions throughout the package. Accurate evaluation of such multi-block thermal systems is essential for ensuring reliability, preventing localized thermal hot spots, and enabling thermal-aware design decisions. High-fidelity numerical solvers such as the finite-element method (FEM) and finite-volume method (FVM) are widely used for package-level thermal analysis in tools such as Ansys IcePak, Cadence Celsius, and COMSOL [3]–[8]. While these methods provide accurate solutions to the governing heat equations, their computational cost grows rapidly with mesh resolution, material heterogeneity, and system size. With the rapid evolution of machine learning, data-driven and physics-informed approaches have attracted growing atten-tion for thermal modeling. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) [9] incorporate governing equations into the loss function to improve physical consistency, but remain tied to fixed design configurations and typically require retraining for each design. Operator-learning approaches instead learn mappings between function spaces and have shown promising performance for partial differential equation (PDE) surrogate modeling. For example, DeepOHeat [10] and DeepOHeat-v1 [11] map power and boundary-condition inputs to 3D temperature fields with significant speedups over FEM, while PI-ONet [12] extends this approach to multilayer chiplet stacks. Neural operators such as the Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) [13] and the U-shaped Neural Operator (U-NO) [14] further improve data efficiency and scalability for PDE-based surrogate modeling, enabling significant acceleration of ther-mal simulations [15]. While effective for fixed package configurations, monolithic models have several limitations for heterogeneous package architectures. First, the learned model is strongly tied to the entire package topology, making it difficult to scale trained models across different numbers of dies, memory stacks, and handle changes in interface regions. Second, interface physics between neighboring components is implicitly em-bedded within the model rather than explicitly enforced. As a result, temperature continuity and heat-flux balance across interfaces cannot be monitored or controlled during inference, limiting the model’s physical interpretability. These limitations are particularly problematic for heteroge-neous packages, which naturally consist of multiple physical components with different materials, geometries, and thermal behaviors. Instead of learning a single global predictor, it is desirable to treat each component as an independent thermal block whose behavior can be modeled separately and flexibly composed into larger package systems. Domain decomposition methods (DDM) provide a natural framework for addressing such coupled multi-domain prob-lems. In classical DDM, a large computational domain is par-titioned into smaller subdomains, each solved locally while ex-changing boundary information with neighboring subdomains. Iterative updates of interface boundary conditions ensure that the local solutions converge to a globally consistent solution arXiv:2610.02461v1 [cs.LG] 1 Oct 2026 [16]. Recently, several studies have explored combining neural operators with domain decomposition to accelerate the solu-tion of PDEs [17], [18]. These approaches demonstrate the potential of learned models as local subdomain solvers within an iterative framework. However, existing learning-based DDM approaches are primarily developed for canonical PDE settings, where the global domain is partitioned into reusable local subproblems or congruent subdomains. Their main focus is geometry general-ization and efficient local surrogate reuse, rather than modular thermal assembly of heterogeneous blocks. In contrast, 3D-IC packages are inherently compositional thermal systems con-sisting of different blocks. These blocks may exhibit different materials, internal structures, and modeling requirements while being strongly coupled through shared interfaces. Modeling such systems therefore requires explicit handling of interface coupling as well as the ability to combine different solver types within a unified framework. In this work, we adopt a compositional perspective and for-mulate heterogeneous package thermal analysis as a modular multi-block system. Each physical component is represented by an independent block-level thermal predictor, while global thermal consistency is enforced through iterative exchange of interface boundary conditions among adjacent blocks. This formulation allows flexible composition of heterogeneous blocks when simulating larger package assemblies, and sup-ports reusable component-level models. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:  

> •

We formulate heterogeneous multi-block thermal analysis as a compositional thermal modeling framework, where each physical component (e.g., dies, HBMs, interposers) is represented by an independent block-level thermal predictor connected through physical interfaces. This for-mulation enables flexible composition of heterogeneous blocks into larger package assemblies, avoiding the tight coupling inherent in monolithic AI models that learn the entire package as a single predictor.  

> •

We propose an interface-iterative thermal solving scheme that explicitly exchanges temperature and heat-flux boundary conditions between neighboring blocks. The iterative formulation enables controllable trade-offs be-tween solution accuracy and runtime by adjusting the iteration budget, while enforcing temperature continuity and heat-flux balance across interfaces. B. Block Decomposition and Interface Coupling 

To enable modular thermal solving, the global domain is decomposed into individual blocks that are solved locally. Each block receives boundary information from its neighbor-ing blocks through shared interfaces. For each interface Γij 

between two blocks, the temperature and heat flux are ex-changed to serve as updated boundary conditions for the next iteration. At iteration k, each block receives interface boundary conditions computed from its neighbors during iteration k − 1.To be specific, in the proposed coupling scheme, temperature at the interface is passed from one block to its neighbor as a Dirichlet boundary condition, while the receiving block returns the corresponding heat flux at the same interface.

D. Local Block Solvers 

Within the proposed framework, each block is solved inde-pendently using a local solver, which is an AI-based surrogate model in this work. The local solver takes the following as input:  

> •

power distribution within the block  

> •

interface boundary conditions from neighboring blocks  

> •

block-specific geometry and material parameters The solver outputs updated interface quantities (temperature or heat flux) after solving the local thermal problem. In this work, AI-based local solvers are instantiated using a neural operator architecture. Specifically, we employ the U-NO [14] to approximate the mapping between block inputs and interface responses. The proposed framework is not tied to a specific model architecture, and other surrogate models (e.g., CNN-based models or physics-informed networks) can also serve as local block solvers within the same interface-iterative procedure. 

E. Block Representation 

To reduce modeling complexity while preserving dominant thermal behavior, each block is represented using a simplified layered structure consisting of a substrate layer, a device layer, and an interconnect layer. The interconnect layer aggregates multiple back-end-of-line (BEOL) metal and via layers into a single effective thermal layer. This representation captures the dominant vertical thermal resistance while maintaining manageable input dimensionality for the learned solver.

A. Experimental Setup 

Case Studies and Configurations. We evaluate DAIST on two representative 2.5D/3D-IC assemblies: the multi-chiplet system (Interposer–SoC–HBM) and the advanced packaging system (Interposer–MC–TIM), as depicted in Figure 2. In the multi-chiplet system, multiple SoC and HBM stacks are mounted on a shared interposer in a flip-chip assembly, with SoC and HBM dies attached face-down to the face-up interposer. To demonstrate composability, SoC instance counts are sampled uniformly from {1, 2, 3} and HBM counts are sampled uniformly from {1, . . . , 10 }. This sampling strat-egy produces assemblies with substantially diverse thermal footprints and interface coupling complexity. In the advanced packaging system, the molding compound (MC) is positioned face-down and covers the region of interest on the interposer, while the thermal interface material (TIM) is placed above the MC. Section II (substrate, device, interconnect). Layer thicknesses, conductivities k, and volumetric heat generation follow Ta-ble I. Each layer follows a 50 × 50 in-plane discretization. 

Boundary Conditions. Adiabatic (Neumann) conditions with zero normal heat flux are imposed on all exterior lateral faces. For both assemblies, top and bottom surfaces have a heat transfer coefficient (HTC) of h = 100 W/(m 2·K), with the ex-ception of the top surface of the interposer, which is adiabatic. For all surfaces with HTC, we use a fixed ambient temperature 

T∞ = 298 .15 K. Internal block-to-block interfaces follow the iterative Dirichlet/Neumann exchange described in Section II without separate contact resistances. 

Data Generation. For each block type (interposer, SoC, HBM, MC, TIM), a separate supervised dataset is generated with geometric parameters consistent with Table I. In the multi-chiplet system, power is generated exclusively in the SoC and HBM blocks, with the interposer carrying no internal heat sources. Power maps are sampled from Gaussian random fields (GRFs) with length-scale parameter 0.3. In the advanced packaging system, the interposer and TIM have no internal heat sources, and the MC block contains 1–4 embedded power regions at random locations. Each power region has a lateral dimension of 1 mm × 1 mm, with power profiles and interface boundary conditions sampled from the same GRF as described above. Each block dataset contains 5,000 total samples. For each sample, ground-truth fields are obtained from the Celsius Thermal Solver (abbreviated as Celsius). Footprints, layer thicknesses, conductivities, and external boundary conditions can all be treated as input variables for training. For simplicity, these variables are fixed in this work because variations in power, internal interface boundary conditions, and topology are the primary focus. 

Training. For each block dataset, a ratio of 8:1:1 is used for splitting into training, validation, and test sets. This yields 4,000 training, 500 validation, and 500 test samples per block type. The interface relaxation parameters are set to ωT = 0 .5

for temperature and ωq = 0.5 for heat flux, which are selected via grid search on the validation set. Each model is trained for 1,000 epochs on 4 NVIDIA A100 GPUs, taking approximately 3 hours per block type. Training all five block types (interposer, SoC, HBM, MC, TIM) requires 15 hours in total, but these models are subsequently reused across any topology without retraining. By contrast, a monolithic predictor requires a full training run for each new package configuration, as its learned mapping is tied to a specific die count and arrangement. 

Compared Methods. DAIST results were compared against the Celsius Thermal Solver and a monolithic neural predic-tor (ML baseline). Existing multi-block ML baselines for thermal simulation are limited. For example, single-block methods such as DeepOHeat-v1 [11] and PI-ONet [12] address fixed single-domain prediction and cannot extend to variable-topology multi-block assemblies without fundamental archi-tectural changes and full retraining. Therefore, a monolithic U-NO predictor is adopted in this work as the ML baseline for multi-block thermal analysis. It is the most direct neural alternative to DAIST for full-package prediction, and it uses an identical architecture and hyperparameters to DAIST’s per-block models. A detailed description and analysis of this baseline is provided in Section III-C.

C. Comparison with Monolithic Neural Predictor 

DAIST is evaluated against the monolithic neural predictor, the most direct ML baseline for full-package thermal predic-tion, on the multi-chiplet system. This comparison focuses on whether compositional decomposition preserves in-distribution accuracy while enabling generalization to unseen topologies. 

Setup. A fixed-topology dataset of 5,000 cases is constructed with the 2 SoC + 8 HBM configuration, and split by 8:1:1 into training, validation, and test sets. Locations and power maps of SoC and HBM blocks are randomized across all samples. Both DAIST and the monolithic predictor are trained using this dataset. This DAIST instance is trained under the same fixed-topology constraint as the monolithic predictor. It is distinct from the general DAIST evaluated in Section III-B, which is trained across variable topology counts. Accuracy metrics between the two subsections are therefore not directly comparable. The monolithic predictor uses an identical U-NO architec-ture and hyperparameters to each per-block model in DAIST. Its inputs are the SoC power maps, HBM power maps, SoC footprint masks, HBM footprint masks, and external boundary conditions. Its output is the interposer top-surface temperature field. SoC and HBM masks are kept separate because they differ in height, power range, and thermal pathways. Notably, the monolithic predictor receives strictly richer input than any individual DAIST block model, including full power maps and spatial masks for all dies. This gives the monolithic predictor an information advantage per forward pass. DAIST’s per-block models each receive only their local power map and interface boundary conditions, with no global topology information. This makes the comparison conservative for DAIST. If the monolithic predictor still fails on unseen topologies despite having access to more information, the failure is structural and cannot be attributed to any information asymmetry favoring DAIST. 

Evaluation. Both methods are evaluated on two test sets of 100 cases each:  

> •

Fixed-topology : held-out cases from the same 2 SoC + 8 HBM distribution (in-distribution).  

> •

Variable-topology : cases drawn from the general multi-chiplet distribution in Section III-A, where SoC count is sampled uniformly from {1, 2, 3} and HBM count from 

{1, . . . , 10 }. This test set is out-of-distribution for both 

methods, as neither was retrained on variable topologies. Although DAIST predicts temperature fields for all blocks, we report only the interposer top-surface temperature for fair comparison, as this is the only quantity both methods produce. Results are reported in Table IV. 

In-Distribution Performance. On the fixed-topology test set, DAIST achieves lower MAE ( 0.282 K vs. 0.433 K) than the monolithic predictor. The monolithic predictor is faster at inference ( 0.12 s vs. 0.82 s) since it requires no iterative inter-face coupling. Both methods are accurate enough for practical use on the trained configuration, confirming that iterative decomposition does not sacrifice in-distribution accuracy. The monolithic predictor’s lower latency stems from the absence of iterative coupling overhead. Users with fixed topology and in-distribution operating conditions may prefer this increased speed in exchange for less flexibility. Conversely, DAIST’s iterative overhead is the necessary cost to achieve topology-agnostic generalization. 

Out-of-Distribution Generalization. The critical distinction emerges on the variable-topology test set. DAIST’s MAE increases modestly from 0.282 K to 0.351 K (24% relative degradation). The monolithic predictor degrades catastroph-ically, with MAE rising from 0.433 K to 26 .264 K, a 60 ×

increase showing its inability in generalization tasks. This performance distinction arises from a fundamental structural difference. The monolithic predictor maps the power distributions of exactly 2 SoC and 8 HBM dies to a full-package temperature field. When the die count changes, it receives inputs of a different structure than it was trained on and fails to generalize. DAIST decomposes the problem along physical block boundaries, and each block model receives only its local power map and the interface boundary conditions from neighboring blocks. Neither the SoC nor the HBM model has information about how many sibling dies exist on the interposer, but rather they just respond to the flux or temperature at interfaces. The interposer model likewise receives flux contributions from whatever blocks contact it, regardless of their count or arrangement. In the multi-chiplet sys-tem, the interposer receives heat from multiple discrete die footprints with independently sampled power maps, producing a distributed thermal load across its surface. Closing this domain gap through lightweight fine-tuning on a small number of advanced packaging samples is a natural direction for future work. While accuracy decreases relative to the in-distribution multi-chiplet result ( 0.253 K MAE), this reflects the cost of zero-retraining cross-topology reuse. Specifically, the interposer model generalizes to a fundamentally different stack (from multiple chiplets to a single MC) without any exposure to MC–TIM training data. 

Convergence and Speedup. With a predefined convergence criterion of 0.1%, DAIST requires on average 15 .3 interface iterations to converge for the three-block sequential chain (interposer → MC → TIM). This higher iteration count relative to the multi-chiplet case ( 5.2 iterations) is due to the sequential topology of the assembly. The interposer has no direct interface with the TIM block, so thermal information must propagate through an intermediate MC block before the full chain reaches consistency. We presented DAIST, a composable thermal solver that decomposes heterogeneous 2.5D/3D-IC packages into modular blocks, replaces subdomain solvers with neural operators, and couples them through iterative interface exchange of temper-ature and heat flux. Evaluated on a multi-chiplet system and an advanced packaging system, DAIST achieves up to 178× speedup over Celsius with mean temperature errors of 0.068% and 0.323%, respectively. Notably, the advanced packaging result is achieved with a frozen interposer model transferred directly from the multi-chiplet system, demonstrating cross-topology reuse of block-level models without retraining. Fu-ture work will explore transfer learning to narrow the cross-topology accuracy gap, extend the framework to more complex 3D-IC packages, and apply DAIST to thermal-aware design optimization.
